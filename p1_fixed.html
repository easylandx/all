The six patterns

Every landing page is already using behavioral psychology. Most just do it by accident.

easylandx builds these six patterns into the blocks themselves, so your page is arguing your case even when you're not in the room.

01

Nothing is judged alone

The relativity trap

Visitors don't know what your product is worth. They only know what it's worth next to the other options on the page — which is why a bare price, shown with nothing to compare it to, is the weakest thing you can put on a landing page.

In easylandxPricing and feature blocks ship with a built-in comparison layout, so your best plan is always shown beside a deliberate decoy — not floating alone.
02

"Free" isn't a price, it's an emotion

The zero-price effect

€1 and €0 are not one euro apart. Free triggers a different part of the brain — visitors will hand over an email, sit through a demo, or start a trial for something free that they'd never pay a cent for.

In easylandxThe lead-capture block is tuned around a free-first offer — free trial, free template, free audit — with the paid step introduced only after that first "yes."
03

The first number sets the ceiling

Anchoring

Whatever price a visitor sees first becomes the yardstick for every price after it. Show the highest plan first, or a "usually €199" struck through, and every number after it gets judged against that anchor — not against some absolute sense of fair value.

In easylandxThe pricing block defaults to showing your premium tier and any "was / now" pricing above the fold, so the anchor is set before visitors reach your actual offer.
04

Warm copy and a hard sell can't share a page

Social vs. market norms

A landing page that sounds like a friend giving advice, then suddenly hits with "BUY NOW — LIMITED STOCK," breaks the spell instantly. Once market logic shows up, the social warmth is gone for good — visitors read the rest of the page as a transaction, not a relationship.

In easylandxCopy blocks are split into "social" sections (story, testimonials, guides) and "market" sections (pricing, checkout), styled distinctly so the tone shift is deliberate, not accidental.
05

Let them touch it before they buy it

The endowment effect

The moment someone customizes something — drags a block, picks a color, previews their own logo on it — they start valuing it like it's already theirs. Losing it now costs more, psychologically, than paying for it would.

In easylandxThe builder lets visitors edit a live preview of their own page before any signup — by the time the paywall appears, they're not buying a tool, they're keeping a page they've already made.
06

Your visitor's future self needs help now

Procrastination & self-control

Visitors arrive calm and rational, fully intending to "come back later." Later rarely comes. The page has to do the committing for them, in the moment, while they're still willing.

In easylandxCountdown blocks, one-click publish, and autosaved drafts turn a vague intention into a page that's already live — the commitment device does the work self-control usually fails at.

Building a page this week? Check it against three things.

Check 01

Is there a decoy?

If you'd still pick your target plan with the middle option removed, the decoy isn't doing its job — or you don't need three tiers at all.

Check 02

Where's the free step?

Every page needs one zero-cost action before the paid one — a template, a trial, a checklist — or the first ask is too big.

Check 03

What commits them now?

Don't rely on visitors coming back. Give their "cold state" intention a deadline, a draft, or a one-click action before they leave.
