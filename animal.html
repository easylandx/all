From the book

"People who do crazy things are not necessarily crazy." Aronson's central claim was that most behavior isn't random — it's a predictable reaction to the situation someone is placed in.

A landing page is a situation. The layout, the words, the order things appear in — all of it quietly tells a visitor how to feel and what to do next, long before they've consciously decided anything. EasyLandX pages are designed around six of the pillars from Aronson's work, translated from the psychology lab to the thing people actually see when they tap the link in your bio.

Six ideas, one page

The same forces Aronson documented in classrooms and courtrooms are what decide whether someone books, messages, or scrolls past.

01

Conformity & obedience

Why we copy the group — even when we know better

The finding

In the Asch experiments, people gave answers they knew were wrong just to match the group. Aronson traces this to three depths of influence: compliance (for reward), identification (to be like someone), and internalization (a belief that becomes truly yours).

On your page

EasyLandX puts real numbers, reviews and client names above the fold — not as decoration, but because a stranger trusts a page other people have already trusted.

02

Cognitive dissonance

Why confusing pages get closed, not read twice

The finding

Holding two conflicting ideas at once feels uncomfortable. Rather than sit with it, people resolve the discomfort fast — usually by justifying whatever's easiest, including closing the tab.

On your page

One clear offer, one clear price, one clear next step. Every EasyLandX page removes the conflicting signals that give a visitor a reason to leave instead of a reason to act.

03

Persuasion & order

Why what comes first — and what comes last — carries the weight

The finding

Aronson found that fear-based messages only change behavior when paired with a clear, doable action. And information placed first sticks when a decision is delayed; information placed last wins when the decision is immediate.

On your page

The headline earns attention in the first three seconds. The button is the very last thing a visitor sees before they decide — because on a landing page, that decision is almost always immediate.

04

The catharsis myth

Why "more page" is rarely the fix

The finding

Aronson disproved the idea that venting frustration relieves it — acting on a feeling tends to amplify it, not release it. Friction works the same way: giving people more to push through doesn't calm them, it compounds their hesitation.

On your page

No extra steps, no extra forms, no extra scrolling to find the button. EasyLandX pages remove friction instead of adding more page for a visitor to fight through.

05

In-group & belonging

Why "this is for people like me" outperforms "this is for everyone"

The finding

People instinctively sort into "us" and "them," favoring their own group. Aronson's own Jigsaw Classroom showed that giving people a shared goal — not just shared space — is what actually builds trust across a divide.

On your page

Your page speaks directly to your audience, in their language and their world, not a generic template. A visitor should feel like the page was made for exactly them — because with EasyLandX, it was.

06

Attraction & the gain-loss effect

Why proximity, similarity and a rising impression win trust

The finding

We're drawn to what feels close and familiar. And Aronson's gain-loss theory found people respond more warmly to an impression that improves over time than to one that was good from the very start.

On your page

The page opens simply and earns the visitor's confidence section by section, so trust is still climbing right up to the moment they hit the button — never front-loaded and never fading.
