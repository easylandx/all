on what actually restores attention

Some websites feel like traffic. Ours feel like a walk in the park.

Research on attention shows that a city street forces your brain to filter noise, screens, and clutter — while a quiet path lets it settle. Most landing pages are built like the street. We build the path.

One kind of attention drains you. The other refills you.

Directed attention is what a busy street demands — constantly filtering traffic, signage, and noise. It's useful, but it's exhausting, and it burns out fast. Popups, autoplay video, and five competing offers ask a visitor's brain to do the same kind of filtering.

Fascination attention is what a quiet trail invites — the mind tracking something gentle without effort. A page built around one clear path lets a visitor's attention rest into it instead of fighting it.

The page has to earn quiet before it earns a yes.

Deep, uninterrupted stretches of nature let the prefrontal cortex rest enough to think clearly again. A page interrupted every few seconds by a popup or a chat bubble never gives a visitor that stretch.

Take the interruptions out and something simple happens: people actually finish reading, and by the time they reach the button, they've already made up their mind.
