the science of what people read

Your landing page is one half of a conversation.

Brain research shows that a single negative word can spike a reader's stress response before they've even finished the sentence, while warm, deliberate phrasing does the opposite. Most small-business sites are written without any of that in mind. We write and build ours with it front of mind.

Words are not just words.

Brain scans show that a single negative word can trigger the amygdala and flood the body with stress hormones almost instantly. Positive, deliberate language does the opposite — it activates the parts of the brain tied to reward and trust.

Put that on a landing page and the stakes get concrete. "Don't miss out" and "Never lose a customer again" read as urgency, but they land as threat. "See what's possible" and "Keep every customer you've already earned" say something close to the same thing — and land completely differently.

Three positives for every friction point.

A negative moment — a price, a form field, a "sign up" a visitor didn't expect yet — carries more weight in the brain than an equivalent positive one. To keep trust intact, it needs roughly three reassuring signals around it.

So next to your price, we put what's included. Next to the sign-up field, a line on what happens right after. Next to the ask, proof that other people already said yes. It isn't padding — it's what keeps the page from reading as a demand.

Thirty seconds, not three minutes.

Working memory is narrow. Past roughly thirty seconds of continuous information, a listener's brain starts discarding what it just heard — and a reader's eye does the same thing with a wall of text.

So each section of your page carries one idea, said once, in as few words as it needs. If a paragraph can't be read in a breath, we cut it in half rather than ask a visitor to hold more than they can.

Warmly. Slowly. Briefly.

The brain registers tone before it processes the actual words. These three habits shape how every page we write actually reads.

Tone

Copy that sounds like the person who owns the business talking to a customer, not a company addressing a market.

Pace

Enough space between sections that a visitor can take a breath before the page asks for anything else.

Brevity

Short sentences, short headlines, and exactly one call to action doing exactly one job.

Trust comes before the ask.

A wary reader's brain is scanning for reasons not to trust a page before it looks for reasons to act. A real photo, a specific detail, a genuine line from a past client — these lower the guard before the page ever asks for anything.

That's why we build in appreciation before persuasion: what a client already does well, said plainly, before we ever get to the offer.
