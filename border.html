The border

Seeing and thinking are two different systems — and your page has to satisfy both.

Philosopher Ned Block spent decades showing that perception and cognition are not the same process wearing different hats. Perception is fast, rich, and wordless. Cognition is slower, selective, and built from concepts. A landing page fires the first system the instant it loads, and only earns the second if the first one cooperates.

What visitors see

The perceptual layer arrives whole and immediate — color, contrast, motion, shape, spacing — before any concept has been formed about what the business even does.

  • Color palette and contrast
  • Layout rhythm and whitespace
  • Motion and hierarchy of first impression
  • Texture, imagery, shape language

What visitors think

The cognitive layer is slower and effortful — it's where the offer gets understood, trust gets weighed, and the decision to act actually gets made.

  • The headline's promise
  • Pricing, credibility, proof
  • What the button says it will do
  • The decision to click, message, or buy
Overflow

Visitors absorb far more than they can consciously process — design for the glance, not just the read.

Block calls this overflow: conscious perception holds more detail than working memory can ever report or store. A visitor's eye takes in the whole page before their mind can name any part of it. That gap is where a landing page either earns a second look, or loses one.

1 glance

is enough for a visitor to perceive your entire layout, palette, and shape language — before reading a single line.

Few words

make it through the attentive bottleneck into working memory. Everything else in the headline is wasted.

1 button

should be the thing attention is steered toward — the bridge from perceptual impression to cognitive action.
