Four ways most website builders "listen"

You've probably met one of these already

Oscar Trimboli names four listening habits that quietly wreck a conversation. Swap "conversation" for "the call where you explain your business," and they're painfully familiar.

The Template Seller

Interrupts before you finish

Already has a theme picked out before you've explained what your business actually does. Fast, but for the wrong thing.

The Distracted Freelancer

Present, but not really there

Nods along, takes the brief, then builds something that misses half of what you said — because half of it never landed.

The Over-Sympathizer

Agrees with everything, questions nothing

Validates every idea you bring, even the ones that would quietly hurt conversions, instead of gently pushing back.

The Fixer

Jumps to a solution before understanding the goal

Hears "I need a website" and starts building immediately, without ever asking what that website is supposed to achieve.

The five levels we listen at

How a real EasyLandX page actually gets built

Trimboli's listening pyramid moves from the words themselves down to what they actually mean. That's the same path we walk with every client before writing a single line of copy.

01

We set our own assumptions aside

No template bias, no "every business needs this" checklist — we start the call ready to actually hear your business, not slot it into a mold.

02

What you're literally asking for

The explicit brief: what you sell, who buys it, what you think the page needs to say.

03

The situation around it

Your market, your competitors, what's already been tried, and why it didn't quite work.

04

What you're not saying

The hesitation in "I guess that could work," or the detail you mention twice without dwelling on it.

05

What you actually mean

"I want a nicer website" is rarely about aesthetics. Usually it means "I want to be taken seriously." That's what we build for.

The math of a discovery call

Why we don't rush the first conversation

The brain processes thought at roughly 900 words a minute. The mouth manages about 125 to 150. That gap means the first sentence out of anyone's mouth is rarely the whole point — which is exactly why we let the conversation breathe before we start building.

Thinking speed
~900 words/min
Speaking speed
~140 words/min
Our actual discovery-call script

Three prompts, not a sales pitch

Trimboli's advice for closing the thought-to-speech gap is disarmingly simple. These are the same three lines we lean on before recommending anything.

"What else?"

The second answer is usually closer to the real need than the first one.

"And…?"

A small nudge that lets you keep going, instead of us jumping in with a fix.

"Tell me more."

No assumptions filled in on your behalf — just room for the full picture.
