How people actually use the web

Three habits to design around — not fight.

01

They scan

Eyes hunt for anchors — a headline, a button, a bold word. Nobody reads top to bottom.

02

They satisfice

First reasonable option wins. Optimizing for "good enough" beats holding out for "best."

03

They muddle through

Most people never learn how something works. They just find what's worked before and repeat it.

§2

Krug's laws of usability

  • 1

    Don't make me think

    Kill every question mark before it forms. If a user has to wonder, the design already failed.

  • 2

    Mindless clicks don't matter

    Extra steps are fine — as long as each one is obvious. Confusion costs; clicks don't.

  • 3

    Omit needless words

    Cut your copy in half. Then cut what's left in half again.

Building a scannable layout

Convention isn't boring — it's the fastest path to "obvious."

Use conventions

Logo top-left. Search top-right. Fighting habit costs the user, not the designer.

Build hierarchy

Size, weight, and color should map straight to importance — at a glance.

Make clickables obvious

A button should look pressable before anyone hovers it.

Cut the noise

Every extra border, icon, or divider competes with the one thing that matters.

Roadmapping & navigation

Show the anchor

Navigation should always answer: where am I right now?

Name pages honestly

What the link promises is what the header should deliver.

Keep search visible

The one tool people trust when everything else fails — never hide it.

Test it yourself

Early and often

A flaw caught this week is cheap. The same flaw next quarter is not.

Three users is enough

The big problems show up fast — you don't need a lab full of people.

Fix, don't just find

Spend the time you save on repairs, not on cataloguing more issues.
