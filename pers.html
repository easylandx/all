01

Privileged moments

the split-second window where attention decides the outcome

Cialdini's most famous example is a furniture site that changed one thing: the background image. Visitors who saw fluffy clouds went on to prioritize comfort when shopping for a sofa. Visitors who saw a background of pennies prioritized price. Nobody asked them a question — the environment quietly picked their buying criteria for them.

On a landing page, the "background" is everything that loads before your headline registers: the hero image, the first icon, the color your eye lands on. Whatever you put there becomes the lens your visitor reads the rest of the page through.

02

Priming & the psychological chute

the question you ask first decides the answer you get later
The chute

Ask the identity question first

Ask "Do you consider yourself an adventurous person?" before pitching a new product, and the mind goes hunting for proof that the answer is yes. By the time the offer arrives, saying yes to it just keeps the story straight.

Q: Are you the kind of team that ships fast?
Most people quietly answer "yes" in their head. Now show them easylandx's publish-in-minutes flow — they're not being sold speed, they're confirming who they already said they are.
The anchor

Set a ridiculous number first

A consultant who jokes "I won't be charging you a million dollars for this" makes the real invoice feel modest by comparison — before a single feature is discussed.

Show the "agency retainer" number before easylandx's price. The real figure doesn't need to be cheap — it needs to be a relief.

Mechanics of attention

what actually stops the scroll, and what keeps it from leaving
What hijacks a scroll

Novelty, threat, and stakes

Human attention is wired to snap toward survival-relevant cues: danger, novelty, anything unresolved. A hero section that promises a real stake — a deadline, a risk avoided, a genuinely new idea — earns the first three seconds. A generic "welcome to our site" doesn't.

The Zeigarnik effect

An unfinished task is a magnet

The brain dislikes loose ends. A visible, almost-finished checklist creates a pull toward closure that a paragraph of copy never will.

04

Unity — the 7th principle

people don't just like you, they can feel like they made this with you

Cialdini's original six weapons of influence were Reciprocity, Liking, Social Proof, Authority, Scarcity, and Consistency. In Pre-Suasion he adds a seventh: Unity — the feeling of belonging to the same "we." Ask someone their opinion and they evaluate you from the outside. Ask for their advice and they step inside the project as a co-creator.

Judged from outside

Asks for an opinion
